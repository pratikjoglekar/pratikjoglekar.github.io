PROJECT 1: Coursera for Campus — Native LTI Integrations
===============================================================
 
ROLE: Sole Product Designer
TEAM: 1 Designer, 8 Developers, 1 PM, 1 Data Analyst
DURATION: Ongoing (started June 2025)
DISCIPLINES: UXR, Design Strategy, System Design, UX, UI
 
SCALE & CONTEXT:
- Enterprise platform serving institutional LMS integrations across ~13,000 enterprise accounts.
- Replaces SchoolDay (GG4L) third-party middleware with a first-party LTI capability inside Canvas, Blackboard, and Moodle.
 
PROBLEM:
- 10% successful LMS connection rate.
- Middleware dependency limited reliability, observability, and product velocity.
- 57% of enterprise admin issues escalated to support.
 
FRAMING SHIFT:
- Initial framing: "ship a working LMS integration."
- Reframed as: "treat integrations as a first-class, first-party platform capability."
 
TRIGGER MOMENTS:
- Sync failures (grade passback, activity sync) were impossible to diagnose because Coursera had no observability into middleware.
- Faculty workflows did not map cleanly to LMS curricula, forcing manual workarounds.
- Setup overhead was blocking enterprise GTM motion.
 
KEY DECISIONS:
 
  Decision: Build native LTI rather than continue institution-specific custom setups.
  Why: Custom integrations created operational overhead and capped scalability.
  Tradeoff: Less flexibility for edge-case LMS configurations.
  Mitigation: Broad LTI standards support + bounded configuration for institutional needs.
 
  Decision: Real-time grade and enrollment sync rather than periodic batch.
  Why: Delayed records eroded instructor and admin trust.
  Tradeoff: Higher technical complexity and dependency on LMS reliability.
  Mitigation: Clear error states, retries, transparent sync status.
 
  Decision: Opinionated, LTI-standards-aligned flow over exposing all configuration upfront.
  Why: Most institutions share core needs; over-flexibility increased error rates and support load.
  Tradeoff: Power users had fewer customization options.
  Mitigation: Progressive disclosure of advanced settings; documented supported extensions.
 
METHODS USED:
- AI-assisted prototyping for rapid end-to-end concept under GTM time pressure.
- Focused group with CSMs, Implementation Managers, and Solutions Architects to surface frontline pain.
- MVP usability testing.
- Deep-dive customer interviews on personalization, AI, search/browse, and decision support.
- Cross-functional workshop to prioritize against user impact, feasibility, and platform leverage.

THE FOUR MIDDLEWARE PROBLEMS (as told in the case study):
- Late, opaque grades: grade sync lagged by a day or two, and failures were hard to diagnose, so faculty could not trust the gradebook for credit.
- Slow, manual setup: onboarding was high-touch and often took two to three weeks of back-and-forth.
- Missing academic workflows: universities relied on customizations (multiple LMS shells, granular grade sync, roster-based access); replacing the middleware without them would break existing partners.
- Disconnected learner experience: Coursera content opened in a separate browser tab.

FOCUS GROUP (September 2025):
- Eight Customer Success Managers, Implementation Managers and Solutions Architects across LATAM, EMEA and APAC.
- Findings and what each shaped: embedding Coursera in the LMS was the most requested feature (shaped the embedded learner view); grade sync delays and a need for diagnostics (shaped real-time sync, registration status and the grade-sync cutoff); instructors wanted course detail before selecting and admin and instructor experiences felt far apart (shaped the content picker and admin control); setup needed self-serve guidance (shaped the guided registration flow).
- A survey of admins and instructors among early users followed in March 2026.

STRATEGY — PARITY FIRST:
- Top university partners would not migrate until the native tool matched the workflows they already had, so the roadmap protected existing customers before adding anything new.
- Sequence: core integration (registration, content linking, single sign-in, course-level grade sync, rostering; first customer live December 2025), then one-click setup with a single URL, then academic customizations (granular grade sync, incremental sync, restoring grades for re-enrolled students), then content discovery, then the embedded learner experience.

DESIGN DECISIONS IN DEPTH:
- Admin control over what instructors see: an org-level control opens program selection and only turns on once an active program is saved, so "control on, nothing visible" cannot happen. Each LTI registration shows a status in words (Configured, Not configured, Needs attention), with a warning when enabling with unconfigured registrations. Existing links keep working.
- Grade-sync cutoff: sync was continuous and the LMS did not send due dates, so instructors can set an optional date when creating a link, labeled "Stop grade sync to Gradebook" so it is not mistaken for the LMS due date.
- In the shipped link setup ("Finalize Setup"), the cutoff is a date and time based on the instructor's local time, described as a grade sync cutoff that is NOT the LMS due date; at launch it could not be changed once set. The same screen asks for the grade configuration: final grade sync or item-level grade sync.
- Phase 2, after check-ins with Customer Success Managers: an instructor overview for each LMS course lists every Coursera item with its created date, its "Stop Grade Sync on" date (or "No date was set", or "Ungraded"), and enrolled learners, and lets instructors update the stop date, view learner details or delete a link.
- Program Access Control in practice: turning the toggle on opens a "Program Access Control Enabled" dialog that goes straight to selecting programs; once saved, a green confirmation explains that only selected programs are available in the LMS. Turning it off asks for confirmation, since every program becomes visible again.
- Content linking: choose license scope, then content type; one Specialization is one link with course order visible but no course picker, protecting the one-link, one-grade model.
- Progress and certificates appear only for deep-linked items; instructors can view certificates but not download them.
- Embedded learner view adapts to LMS hosts that scale responsively and to hosts that only allow fixed-size pop-ups.

WHERE IT GOT HARD:
- Each LMS behaves differently (Canvas decides multi-select per placement; Moodle adds its own confirmation page), and most expose little data, so the design degrades gracefully per placement.
- Customers would only migrate between academic semesters, and migration depended on getting enrollment and grade data out of the old middleware.
- Self-serve alone was not enough: customers often still needed hands-on help, so field-team ownership mattered as much as the product.

ATTRIBUTION (Pratik is careful here):
- Real-time sync and the cutoff most plausibly affected instructor trust in grades (survey comments, no metric yet).
- Admin control and registration status plausibly affected support dependency (57% to 7%), and guided registration plausibly affected the connection rate (10% to 31%), but both are hypotheses, not isolated from other changes.

OPTIONS RULED OUT:
- Keep patching the middleware: Coursera would never own observability, grade sync or the roadmap.
- Batch grade updates: delayed grades undermined trust for credit.
- Expose every setting up front: extra flexibility raised error rates and support load.
- Let instructors pick single courses inside a Specialization: it would break the one-link, one-grade model.

LESSON:
- Self-serve is a product plus an operating model: the setup flow is half of it; the other half is what happens when setup fails (visible status, diagnostics, a clear handoff to a person).
 
OUTCOMES:
- LMS connection success: 10% → 31%.
- Support dependency: 57% → 7% over 2 quarters.
 
ORGANIZATIONAL IMPACT:
- Repositioned integrations as durable, owned infrastructure rather than an external add-on.
- Reduced third-party dependency and increased velocity of future integration work.
 
OPEN QUESTIONS / NEXT:
- Final visual design is in flight.
- Continued expansion of LMS coverage and observability.

PROJECT 2: Usage Intelligence Platform — HubSpot
===============================================================
 
ROLE: Sole Product Designer
TEAM: 1 Designer, 4 Developers, 1 PM, 1 Data Analyst
DURATION: 5 months
DISCIPLINES: UXR, Design Strategy, System Design, UX, UI
 
SCALE & CONTEXT:
- Internal usage intelligence and governance platform for HubSpot's SMB customers.
- Cross-functional with Product, Engineering, and Data.
- Affects reporting integrity and downstream decision reliability across product and customer teams.
 
PROBLEM:
- 8.5% expected critical usage gap rate.
- Fragmented event ownership; event logic lived in code files with unclear accountability.
- Reactive incident handling — failures often surfaced only after propagating to external analytics tools.
- Existing exploration tools optimized for analysis, not governance or action.
 
FRAMING SHIFT:
- Initial framing: "improve analytics visibility."
- Reframed as: "establish event ownership and governance — a complementary layer, not a replacement for analytics tools."
 
TRIGGER MOMENTS:
- Multiple anomaly incidents had no accountable owner.
- Deleting a code file silently deleted live event data with no safeguard or signal.
- Failures persisted despite the existence of exploration tooling.
 
KEY DECISIONS:
 
  Decision: Reject building another analytics surface; build governance and ownership instead.
  Why: Existing tools optimized for exploration, not accountability.
  Tradeoff: Less flexibility for ad-hoc analysis.
  Mitigation: Position as complementary; coexist with Amplitude/Looker.
 
  Decision: Flexible event schema over exhaustive upfront standardization.
  Why: Teams needed to move fast; heavy governance would block adoption.
  Tradeoff: Risk of inconsistency early on.
  Mitigation: Validation, auditing, and alerting as safety nets.
 
METHODS USED:
- 8 SMB customer interviews on data experience and gaps.
- Cross-functional workshop to map current system, surface technical constraints, and align on direction.
- Stakeholder alignment on ownership model and decision principles.
 
OUTCOMES:
- Critical usage gap reduced 8.5% → 5.1% (40% improvement, exceeding target).
- Errors in the event creation flow −70% (by removing decision points users did not need).
- No-code event creation enabling faster instrumentation.
- Audit trails and ownership tracking that persisted beyond launch.
 
ORGANIZATIONAL IMPACT:
- Shifted teams from reactive investigation to proactive monitoring.
- Introduced shared event ownership and audit trails.
- Governance persisted beyond the initial rollout.
 
DESIGN DETAIL (from the case study):
- Interview findings and what each shaped: unclear ownership of code-based events (event ownership and an audit trail); deleting a file lost live data (protected events); unannounced changes (notifications when events are created, changed or deleted); no anomaly detection (team alerts); key events hard to find (a usage library with pinned events and volume trends).
- No-code event creation is a four-step flow (event details, properties, reviewers, review). It builds in the naming standard (a noun plus a past-tense verb, such as "Deal Created") and asks up front whether the event is protected.
- Each event's detail page shows its expected range, flags anomalies on the usage chart, names the owning teams, and lets teams subscribe; changes go through reviewers and each event keeps a history.
- The History tab lists every change with its date, who made it and the details (collaborating teams added, properties added, description, protected status, creation). Teams can mark an intended volume change as an "expected drop" and record the reason, so it is not mistaken for an incident.
- Pratik reframed the brief from "improve analytics visibility" to ownership and governance, ran a cross-functional workshop to map the system and its constraints, and aligned stakeholders on an ownership model before deep design.
- Attribution: the drop in critical usage situations (8.5% to 5.1%) is plausibly linked to alerts, protection and ownership, but Pratik treats that as a hypothesis, not isolated from other changes.

OPTIONS RULED OUT:
- Another analytics dashboard: existing tools were built for exploration, not accountability or action.
- Replacing Amplitude and Looker: it would disrupt established workflows and slow adoption.
- Exhaustive standardization up front: heavy governance would have blocked early adoption.

VALIDATION:
- Direction, before building: the 8 SMB interviews and the cross-functional workshop tested governance over another dashboard before design went deep.
- Result, against a target: critical usage situations were tracked against the expected 8.5% and came in at 5.1%, beating the target.
- Whether it lasted: event ownership and audit trails stayed in use after the initial rollout.
- Not measured: how much alerts, protection and no-code creation each contributed on their own.

COUNTERFACTUAL:
- If governance had been ignored: event inconsistencies would have scaled, support burden would have grown, and trust in analytics would have eroded.

PROJECT 3: Retention Intervention at Cancellation — HubSpot
===============================================================
 
ROLE: Sole Product Designer
TEAM: 1 Designer, 2 Developers, 1 PM, 1 Data Analyst
DURATION: 2 months
DISCIPLINES: UXR, Design Strategy, Data Analysis, UX, UI
 
SCALE & CONTEXT:
- SaaS subscription product with early churn breaking starter unit economics.
- CAC payback period was approaching ~2 years; customers churning before payback created negative unit economics.
 
PROBLEM:
- Churn was framed behaviorally rather than economically.
- Starter customers were leaving before reaching CAC payback.
- Risk of permanent pricing changes if handled bluntly.
 
FRAMING SHIFT:
- Reframed from "reduce churn" to "improve dollar retention and lifetime value."
- Anchored success on dollar retention and post-discount continuation, not cancellation deferral.
 
KEY DECISIONS:
 
  Decision: Frame around unit economics and LTV, not raw churn reduction.
  Why: Churn alone masked the true business risk — leaving before CAC payback.
  Tradeoff: Some retained users would still churn later, hurting short-term churn optics.
  Mitigation: Measure success via dollar retention and post-discount continuation.
 
  Decision: Time-bound discount only at cancellation, not earlier in the journey.
  Why: Cancellation is the moment of explicit intent and maximum price sensitivity.
  Tradeoff: No reach for users who churn silently.
  Mitigation: Tightly scoped experiment to validate impact before broadening signals.
 
  Decision: Constrain experiment to a narrowly defined, high-risk starter cohort (Starter Customer Platform, seats-based, monthly commit / monthly pay).
  Why: Reduce noise, increase causal confidence.
  Tradeoff: Results don't immediately generalize.
  Mitigation: Treat as validation; define expansion criteria upfront.
 
METHODS USED:
- Competitive analysis across VEED.IO, Jasper.ai, Notion, Intercom, ClickFunnels, Canva.
- Cohort analysis with data analyst.
- End-to-end cancellation flow mapping to identify the right intervention moment.
- Mini-checkout with immediate discount visibility and clear legal language.
 
DOWNSIDE RISK & INVALIDATION:
- Risk of conditioning price sensitivity.
- Risk of short-term revenue distortion.
- Invalidation criteria: post-discount continuation below 5%, or LTV failing to exceed CAC.
 
OUTCOMES:
- 36% dollar retention among discount recipients.
- 14% post-discount continuation (sustained product usage beyond the discount window).
- Repeatable, cohort-based retention experimentation model.
 
ORGANIZATIONAL IMPACT:
- Shifted retention framing toward economics rather than promotion.
- Established a governed retention experimentation model that could be applied to similar segments.
 
DESIGN DETAIL (from the case study):
- Competitor patterns and what each shaped: offers appear at moments of intent (the offer appears only in the cancellation flow); framing changes by touchpoint (copy written for someone who has decided to leave); supportive language gets accepted (an offer that reads as help, not a hard sell); discounts apply immediately (a breakdown that shows the new price straight away).
- The offer appears at step 2 of 4 in the cancellation flow: 50% off for the next three months, the old and new price side by side, the end date, and a plain statement that it covers existing products and seats only.
- An "offer breakdown" panel lists line by line what is eligible for the discount, what is not, and the new total, so the bill is never a surprise.
- Hypothesis: a 50% discount keeps about a third of price-sensitive customers, who keep paying afterwards. Failure line: post-discount continuation below 5%, or lifetime value not exceeding acquisition cost. The 14% continuation is well above that line.
- Pratik coordinated growth, pricing, legal, engineering and customer experience, and iterated customer-facing language with legal during development.
- The call Pratik made: reframed "reduce churn" around dollar retention and lifetime value, and anchored success on whether customers kept paying after the discount ended, so deferred cancellations were not counted as wins.

OPTIONS RULED OUT:
- A permanent price cut: it changes the price for everyone, including customers happy to pay.
- An offer earlier in the journey: without explicit intent to cancel, the offer is less relevant.
- Rolling out to every customer at once: more noise and less confidence in what caused the result.
- Counting deferred cancellations as wins: it hides churn that simply arrives later.

WHAT I'D DO DIFFERENTLY:
- Systematically A/B test discount levels. The 50% rate validated the hypothesis but optimal threshold was not isolated.

PROJECT 4: Dashboard Redesign — MassMutual
===============================================================
 
ROLE: Senior Product Designer, FinTech (sole designer on this surface)
TEAM: 1 Designer, 7 Developers, 1 Product Owner, 1 Business Analyst, 1 Data Analyst
DURATION: 6 months
DISCIPLINES: UXR, Design Strategy, Data Analysis, UX, UI
 
SCALE & CONTEXT:
- Insurance platform homepage serving as the primary entry point for life insurance and retirement product policyholders.
- Customer portfolios growing in complexity (multiple products, coverage types, beneficiaries).
 
PROBLEM:
- Navigation-heavy homepage; outdated design system limiting scannability.
- Engagement time: 15.02 seconds.
- Mental model confusion across policy data.
- Information depth was insufficient; "face value" wasn't the metric customers cared most about.
 
FRAMING SHIFT:
- Reframed from "navigational dashboard" to "decision-making control surface."
 
TRIGGER MOMENTS:
- 9 of 10 customers found quick links unhelpful — they introduced steps rather than removing them.
- 8 of 10 wanted to learn about their policies directly from the homepage.
- 9 of 10 wanted payment context alongside richer policy details.
 
KEY DECISIONS:
 
  Decision: Treat the homepage as a decision-making control surface, not a navigational dashboard.
  Why: Research showed users logged in to complete financial and policy-critical tasks, not to browse.
  Tradeoff: Reduced emphasis on secondary features and quick links.
  Mitigation: Support deeper exploration contextually.
 
  Decision: Full redesign rather than incremental visual fixes.
  Why: The existing information model could not support growing policy complexity.
  Tradeoff: Higher upfront cost and longer delivery timeline.
  Mitigation: Anchor the work in reusable patterns and scalable components.
 
  Decision: Select Northstar Design B over Design A.
  Why: Design B better guided users through complex policy info (ease-of-use 7.85/8 vs 6.6/8).
  Tradeoff: Required hierarchy tuning to improve discoverability of key actions.
  Mitigation: Refined layout, placement, emphasis based on usability feedback.
 
METHODS USED:
- Quantitative VOC survey with 4.7k customers — 75% Billing & payments, 50% Policy info, 50% Dividends, 38% Beneficiaries, 25% Loans, 25% Other.
- Existing-design usability study.
- Competitive analysis (industry homepages were navigational dashboards; opportunity to differentiate).
- Northstar usability study comparing two design directions.
 
OUTCOMES:
- Engagement time: 15.02s → 34.66s (more than doubled).
- Resume framing: "user adoption +9%."
- Design patterns reused across additional product surfaces.
 
DESIGN DETAIL (from the case study):
- Before: each policy was a tile showing only its face amount, followed by a list of quick links and calculators.
- After: the homepage opens with a greeting and "What would you like to do?" with the most common tasks one click away (for example change address, view or change beneficiaries, start a disability claim), then a dated "Things to do" list for anything needing attention, then policies grouped by type.
- Usability findings and what each shaped: quick links added steps (tasks started from the homepage itself); customers wanted to learn about policies on the homepage (policy summaries with coverage and benefits, not only face value); customers wanted payment context (payments shown with the policy they belong to).
- Design A issues: the label "People on Policy" was unclear, the summary confused people, the next payment date was hard to find, and a cash value projection graph was not useful. Design B guided people better but some missed "What would you like to do?", so it moved to the top with important status.
- Attribution: the rise in time engaged (15.02s to 34.66s) is good because customers finished tasks on the homepage instead of navigating away; Pratik treats the link to specific design choices as a hypothesis.

OPTIONS RULED OUT:
- Incremental visual fixes: they could not fix the information model underneath.
- Quick links as the main way to act: 9 of 10 customers found them unhelpful.
- Design A as the direction: lower ease of use (6.6/8 against 7.85/8), unclear labels and no clear next payment date.
- A cash value projection graph: customers did not find it actionable.

ORGANIZATIONAL IMPACT:
- Established a decision-oriented homepage philosophy.
- Created a scalable design language extended across policy, product, and transactional experiences.
- Influenced subsequent mobile design system work and term-policy experience.

PROJECT 5: Coursera for Teams Pricing Calculator — Coursera
===============================================================
 
ROLE: Sole Product Designer (the only designer on the project; owned design end to end)
TEAM: Worked with engineering, marketing, and data and analytics
DURATION: Feb 2026 to Jul 2026, concept to A/B readout
DISCIPLINES: UX, UI, Pricing and Conversion Design, Experimentation
STATUS: Shipped to 100% of Coursera for Teams (C4T) traffic.
 
PROBLEM:
- Small-team buyers abandoned the C4T purchase path because they learned the real constraints too late.
- Sticker shock: a large annual total appeared early, with no lower-commitment option beside it.
- Hidden details: payment methods, billing specifics and licensing rules appeared only late in checkout.
- Fear of inflexibility: buyers could not tell how adding, removing or swapping users would work.
- The 5-seat minimum was revealed far down the funnel, where it could end a deal.
 
WHAT I DESIGNED:
- A pricing calculator module on the Teams landing page: pick 5–125 seats, compare quarterly and annual billing, read key policies (10% license rotation limit, mid-cycle add/remove) beside the price, see local currency and payment methods, and email the estimate or continue to checkout.
- Above 125 seats, the price area becomes a guided "Contact sales" panel that keeps the seat count and billing cadence.
- An estimate email system: estimate email (with a limited-time-offer variant), Day 7 and Day 14 reminders, and two offer-expiry reminders.
 
KEY DECISIONS:
 
  Decision: Self-serve pricing for 5–125 licenses; guided "Contact sales" path above that.
  Why: Small teams get a fast answer; larger teams reach sales with intent signals (seat count, cadence).
  Tradeoff: Buyers above the range do not get a number from the module.
  Mitigation: Explain what happens next and carry seat count and cadence into the sales form.
 
  Decision: Ship to 100% of C4T traffic despite an 8% drop in Account page progression.
  Why: Full price transparency cost nothing in conversion (Billing page visits and Teams conversion held steady); the visitors who left early looked budget-mismatched, filtering themselves out before checkout. The +15% Teams conversion lift was directional, not statistically significant, so Pratik does not claim it.
 
INSIGHT:
- Teams buyers rarely decide alone; they need a number they trust and can carry to whoever approves the budget.
 
EXPERIMENT:
- 50:50 A/B test, about 5 weeks, randomized by cookie; 7-day attribution window from exposure.
- Exposure to Account page: 12.3% → 11.3% (−8%, statistically significant).
- Exposure to Billing page: 1.4% → 1.4% (+4%, neutral, directional only).
- Exposure to Teams conversion: 0.80% → 0.92% (+15%, neutral, directional only — not claimed as a win).
 
OUTCOMES:
- About 20% of landing page traffic saw the module; 24% of those engaged with it.
- Roughly 75% of calculator users did not continue to the Account page, acting as a low-intent filter.
- Shipped to 100% of C4T traffic.
 
DESIGN DETAIL (from the Figma file and the case study):
- Directions explored: the first direction was a plan comparison ("Find the right plan for your goals"): For Teams (teams of 5-125, a listed price per user per year, Buy now) against For Enterprises (125+, custom price, Contact sales), taken from wireframes through light, blue and dark treatments. It answered which plan fits, not what a team would pay, so it gave way to a calculator.
- Two options were also explored for the Coursera for Business page, each a compact strip that expands into the full calculator: Option 1 started from billing and seat count and switched to enterprise pricing (Contact sales) at high seat counts; Option 2 asked up front whether the buyer wanted Teams or Enterprise, then the billing cadence. Both were decommissioned, and the module shipped on the Coursera for Teams page.
- The shipped module: four plain-language policies on the left (quarterly billing; license transfers of up to 10% per billing cycle; adding licenses anytime with prorated pricing; payment by card, ACH, wire, or purchase order over $10K); in the middle, annual or quarterly billing as two choices with the price per license, a license stepper starting at the 5-license minimum with a volume discounts link, and an Email estimate button; on the right, a Due today summary (taxes included, billing cycle, price per license, number of licenses), the 14-day refund policy and Continue to checkout.
- Email estimate dialog: an email field and Send estimate, so buyers can carry the number to whoever approves the budget.

LEARNINGS / NEXT:
- Judge pricing surfaces by downstream outcomes, not top-of-funnel progression.
- Would design the follow-up (saved estimate, reminders) as part of the module from day one.
- Next: one-click copy or download of the quote; measure estimate and reminder email performance.
